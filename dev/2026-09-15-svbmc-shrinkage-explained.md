# Empirical-Bayes shrinkage of the S-VBMC ELBO, explained

*Written 15 September 2026 as a tutorial companion to the
[headline note](2026-09-15-svbmc-headline-shrinkage.md), which records
the decision, and the
[stage D report](results/2026-09-15-svbmc-pool-comparison.md), which
holds the evidence. It assumes the reader knows what a Gaussian
process, a variational posterior and an ELBO are, and nothing about
VBMC's internals. The reference implementation is
`dev/scripts/svbmc_shrink_elbo.py`; every number quoted here is
regenerated by `python dev/scripts/svbmc_headline_numbers.py` from the
tracked per-subset records, except the worked example of section 7,
which rebuilds three stacks from the run pool.*

## 1. What is being estimated

A VBMC run on a target $p(x, D)$ returns a variational posterior, a
mixture of $K$ Gaussian components ($K = 50$ at the end of a run),

$$
q(x) = \sum_{k=1}^{K} w_k \, q_k(x),
$$

and a Gaussian-process surrogate $f$ of the log joint $\log p(x, D)$
fitted to the evaluations the run made. The run's ELBO is

$$
\mathrm{ELBO} = \mathbb{E}_q[\log p(x, D)] + H[q] \approx G + H,
\qquad G = \sum_{k=1}^{K} w_k I_k ,
$$

where $I_k = \mathbb{E}_{q_k}[f]$ is the expected log joint of component
$k$ under the surrogate, an analytic integral of the GP posterior mean
against a Gaussian, and $H$ is the entropy of the mixture. Since the GP
is a posterior over functions, the vector $(I_1, \dots, I_K)$ has a
posterior distribution too: a Gaussian with a mean and a $K \times K$
covariance, both analytic. VBMC averages over $N_s$ samples of the GP
hyperparameters and stores, with every posterior, the mean under each
sample $s$ as the array `I_sk` ($I_{sk}$, shape $N_s \times K$) and the
covariance under each sample as `J_sjk` ($J_{sjk}$, shape
$N_s \times K \times K$). These two arrays are all that the method
below reads. (Each $I_k$ also carries a fixed
per-component correction for the change of variables between the
constrained space the user sees and the unbounded space VBMC works in;
it is a constant and plays no role in what follows.)

S-VBMC takes $M$ such runs of the same target, puts all their $M K$
components into one mixture and optimizes the weights $w$ of that
mixture to maximize $G + H$, where each component's $I_k$ comes from
its own run's GP and $H$ is the entropy of the stacked mixture,
estimated by Monte Carlo. The stacked ELBO $G + H$ at the optimized
weights is the value the class reports as `raw`.

## 2. Why the raw value is too high on a noisy target

Write each estimate as the true value plus an estimation error,

$$
I_k = \theta_k + \varepsilon_k, \qquad
\varepsilon = (\varepsilon_1, \dots, \varepsilon_K) \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \Sigma),
$$

where $\theta_k$ is what the exact log joint would give and $\Sigma$ is
the covariance the GP assigns to its own estimates (the mean of `J_sjk`
over the hyperparameter samples, plus the spread of `I_sk` across
them). On a noiseless, well-sampled target $\Sigma$ is tiny and
$I_k \approx \theta_k$. On a noisy target the GP is uncertain and the
errors are of the order of half a nat per component.

The weights are then chosen to make $\sum_k w_k I_k$ as large as
possible. Among components of similar true value, the ones whose error
$\varepsilon_k$ happened to come out positive win, so the selected sum
overestimates $\sum_k w_k \theta_k$. This is the winner's curse: pick
the best of many noisy measurements and its measured value is, in
expectation, above its true value. It happens twice. VBMC's own
variational optimization already chose each run's weights on that
run's noisy estimates, which is where most of the optimism comes from;
the stacking then chooses again, among components and among runs,
which adds a smaller part that grows with $M$. On the benchmark's six
noisy targets the raw stacked ELBO sits 0.25 to 0.9 nats above the
truth at $M = 2$ and 0.6 to 1.3 nats above at $M = 16$, and within
0.07 nats on the two noiseless targets. (The truth for a stack is its
own ELBO computed by Monte Carlo from the exact log density, so these
numbers measure estimation error, not the gap between the posterior
and the target.)

## 3. The current headline and where it breaks

The class today reports, for a noisy stack,

$$
\min\bigl(G, \; \operatorname{median}_k I_k\bigr) + H :
$$

the expected log joint is not allowed to exceed the median of the
components' individual estimates. This works when it works because the
low-weight components are the ones the optimization did not select,
so their estimates are unbiased and the median over all components is
a control group. It brings the headline within 0.56 nats of the truth
on five of the six noisy targets at every $M$.

It fails on a heavy-tailed target. The components of a posterior with
heavy tails have very different true values, the ones in the tails
being far below the ones in the core, and the median component is a
tail component. On the 8-dimensional Student-t target the cap lands
0.9 to 1.8 nats below the truth, further from it than the raw value,
and the error grows with $M$. The cap has no notion of how much of the
spread among components is real and how much is noise, which is the
question the next section answers.

## 4. The empirical-Bayes model

Treat the components of one run as a population. Two assumptions:

$$
\theta_k \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \tau^2), \qquad
I_k \mid \theta_k \sim \mathcal{N}(\theta_k, V_k),
$$

that is, the true values scatter around a level $\mu$ with variance
$\tau^2$, and each is measured with a known variance $V_k$, the
diagonal of $\Sigma$. Under this model the posterior mean of the true
value given the measurement is

$$
\mathbb{E}[\theta_k \mid I_k] = \mu + \frac{\tau^2}{\tau^2 + V_k} \, (I_k - \mu).
$$

It is a compromise between the measurement and the population level:
the deviation of $I_k$ from $\mu$ is kept in the proportion of the
population's spread that is real, $\tau^2 / (\tau^2 + V_k)$, and the
rest is attributed to noise. If the measurement is precise
($V_k \ll \tau^2$) the estimate is left alone; if it is noisy relative
to the spread of the population ($V_k \gg \tau^2$) it is pulled to
$\mu$. This is shrinkage in the sense of Stein and of Efron and Morris:
with three or more members in the population and known equal
variances, the James-Stein estimator of this form has lower total
squared error than the raw estimates whatever the true values are, and
the empirical version with estimated $\mu$ and $\tau^2$ and unequal
variances inherits the property approximately.

Why this addresses the selection problem: the weights favour the
components with the largest $I_k - \mu$, and under the model a fraction
$V_k / (\tau^2 + V_k)$ of any such excess is expected to be noise.
Every component is shrunk, selected or not, but the value of the stack
moves because the weights concentrate on the ones that are shrunk
down.

"Empirical" means that $\mu$ and $\tau^2$ are estimated from the same
$K$ values rather than fixed in advance, by the method of moments:

$$
\hat\mu = \frac{1}{K} \sum_k I_k, \qquad
s^2 = \frac{1}{K - 1} \sum_k (I_k - \hat\mu)^2, \qquad
\hat\tau^2 = \max\bigl(s^2 - \nu, \; 0\bigr),
$$

where $\nu$ is the part of the sample variance $s^2$ that estimation
error accounts for (section 5 gives it). If the variance of the
estimates is all noise, $\hat\tau^2$ is zero and every component
collapses to $\hat\mu$; if it is far above the noise, $\hat\tau^2$ is
large and nothing moves. With $K = 50$ components, both moments are
estimated well.

Which population? The run's own components, one run at a time: they
share a GP, a level and a noise scale. Taking every component of the
stack as one population was tried and is worse, because it reads the
different levels of different runs as genuine spread (section 6 treats
those levels separately).

## 5. The errors are correlated, and that matters twice

One GP produces all $K$ estimates of a run, so their errors are
correlated: the mean correlation between two components' errors is
between 0.15 and 0.5 in the runs of section 7. Two things change.

**The noise in the spread.** For independent errors the noise inside
the sample variance of the $I_k$ would be the mean $V_k$. For
correlated errors it is

$$
\nu = \frac{\operatorname{tr} \Sigma - \mathbf{1}^{\top} \Sigma \, \mathbf{1} / K}{K - 1},
$$

with $\mathbf{1}$ the vector of ones, which reduces to the mean $V_k$
when $\Sigma$ is diagonal and is smaller otherwise. The reason: an
error shared by all components moves all the $I_k$ together and adds
nothing to their spread, so it must not be subtracted from it.
Derivation: with $I_k = \theta_k + \varepsilon_k$, take the expectation
of $\sum_k (I_k - \bar I)^2$ over $\varepsilon$ at fixed $\theta$; the
cross terms vanish and

$$
\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\sum_k (\varepsilon_k - \bar\varepsilon)^2\Bigr]
= \operatorname{tr} \Sigma - K \operatorname{Var}(\bar\varepsilon)
= \operatorname{tr} \Sigma - \frac{\mathbf{1}^{\top} \Sigma \, \mathbf{1}}{K},
\qquad \operatorname{Var}(\bar\varepsilon) = \frac{\mathbf{1}^{\top} \Sigma \, \mathbf{1}}{K^2}.
$$

Using the mean $V_k$ instead over-subtracts, so $\hat\tau^2$ comes out
too small and the shrinkage too strong; on the benchmark the
difference is 20 to 50 % of the noise term.

**The shrinkage itself.** With correlated measurement errors the
posterior mean of the whole vector $\theta = (\theta_1, \dots, \theta_K)$
is the matrix version of the formula,

$$
\mathbb{E}[\theta \mid I] = \mu \mathbf{1} + \tau^2 \bigl(\tau^2 \mathrm{Id} + \Sigma\bigr)^{-1} (I - \mu \mathbf{1}),
$$

with $\mathrm{Id}$ the identity matrix, which follows from Gaussian
conditioning with $\operatorname{Cov}(\theta, I) = \tau^2 \mathrm{Id}$
and $\operatorname{Var}(I) = \tau^2 \mathrm{Id} + \Sigma$. It uses the
other components' deviations as evidence about each component's
error. To see what it does, take an exchangeable covariance,

$$
\Sigma = \sigma^2 \bigl[(1 - \rho) \, \mathrm{Id} + \rho \, \mathbf{1} \mathbf{1}^{\top}\bigr].
$$

Its eigenvector $\mathbf{1}$ (a deviation shared by every component)
has eigenvalue $\sigma^2 (1 + (K - 1) \rho)$, large, so along that
direction the factor $\tau^2 / (\tau^2 + \lambda)$ is small: a common
shift of all the estimates is treated as mostly noise. Every contrast
between components has eigenvalue $\sigma^2 (1 - \rho)$, smaller than
$\sigma^2$, so differences between components are kept more than the
diagonal formula would keep them. The report calls the diagonal
version `within` and this one `within full`, and quotes for the
latter the row sums of the shrinkage matrix, the factor a shared
deviation is multiplied by. In the runs of section 7 the diagonal
factor is 0.45 to 0.70 while the row sum is 0.06 to 0.17: the full
form shrinks a common deviation much harder and individual contrasts
somewhat less. On the benchmark at $M = 16$ the full form removes
about a third to two thirds of the optimism of the typical noisy
targets, the diagonal one a tenth to two fifths.

## 6. A second level: selection among runs

After the within-run shrinkage some optimism remains and it grows with
$M$. That part is a selection among runs: the stacking puts its mass
on the runs whose own ELBO came out highest, and the best of more runs
is more optimistic. The within-run populations cannot see it, since a
run's level $\mu$ is what they shrink toward.

So the same model is applied once more, to the $M$ runs as a
population. Each run's level is its own expected log joint under its
own weights $w^{(m)}$, measured with the variance the run's GP assigns
to it (the GP part of the run's own `elbo_sd`):

$$
E_m = \sum_{k \in m} w^{(m)}_k I_k, \qquad
V_m = w^{(m)\top} \Sigma_m \, w^{(m)}.
$$

The levels are shrunk toward their mean over runs with the scalar
formula of section 4 (runs are independent, so their level errors are
uncorrelated and the noise term is the mean $V_m$), and every
component of run $m$ is shifted by the change of its level. The
two-level estimate is the within-run shrinkage plus this shift, and
its value at the stacking's weights is the candidate headline.

With $M$ of 2 to 5, $\hat\tau^2$ for the runs is estimated from that
many values and is crude: it hits zero, which collapses all the levels
to their mean, in a fifth to two thirds of the subsets at $M \le 5$ on
the typical noisy targets. That is the right behaviour when the runs
agree within their noise, which is what such runs do. On the
heavy-tailed target the levels differ by more than their noise,
$\hat\tau^2$ is large and the levels barely move.

## 7. A worked example

Three stacks of $M = 4$ runs from the benchmark's run pool, the first
random subset of each target, rebuilt exactly as the scoring script
rebuilds them. Biases are against the stack's own Monte Carlo ELBO.

| target ($M = 4$, subset 0) | raw | cap | two-level full |
|---|---|---|---|
| multisensory, noise 3 | +0.94 | +0.84 | +0.73 |
| Student-t, 8-D, noise 3 | +0.45 | −1.39 | −0.03 |
| Gaussian mixture, noiseless | −0.00 | −0.04 | −0.00 |

Per run: the standard deviation of the 50 estimates $I_k$ (their
spread, $s$), the mean standard deviation of one estimate (the mean
$\sqrt{V_k}$), the noise share $\nu / s^2$, and the mean shrinkage
factor in the diagonal and the full form.

| run | spread $s$ | mean $\sqrt{V_k}$ | noise share | diag factor | full factor |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| multisensory 0 | 0.79 | 0.62 | 0.51 | 0.45 | 0.06 |
| multisensory 1 | 1.07 | 0.65 | 0.28 | 0.66 | 0.15 |
| multisensory 2 | 0.81 | 0.53 | 0.28 | 0.63 | 0.09 |
| multisensory 3 | 1.06 | 0.60 | 0.26 | 0.70 | 0.17 |
| Student 0 | 1.63 | 0.42 | 0.05 | 0.93 | 0.45 |
| Student 1 | 1.30 | 0.39 | 0.05 | 0.91 | 0.33 |
| Student 2 | 2.86 | 0.61 | 0.04 | 0.95 | 0.67 |
| Student 3 | 1.63 | 0.44 | 0.04 | 0.93 | 0.41 |
| mixture 0 to 3 | 0.49 to 0.76 | ≤ 0.02 | 0.00 | 1.00 | 1.00 |

Reading the rows. On the multisensory target a quarter to a half of
the spread among a run's components is estimation noise, so the
estimates are shrunk hard: the component with the largest stack weight
in run 3 (weight 0.042) sits 1.14 nats above its run's mean with a
standard deviation of 0.47, and is moved to 0.90 above it by the
diagonal formula and to 0.56 by the full one. On the Student target
the spread among components is real (a heavy-tailed posterior's
components differ by nats) and the noise share is 0.05, so the
diagonal formula leaves the estimates almost alone; the full formula
still removes a shared deviation, and the largest component of run 2
(weight 0.18, 2.92 nats above its run's mean) is moved to 2.36. On the
noiseless mixture the GP is nearly exact, the noise share is zero and
nothing moves. The cap, by contrast, would pull the Student stack down
by 1.8 nats because its median component is a tail component.

The run level. The four multisensory runs have levels within 0.35 nats
of one another with a level standard deviation $\sqrt{V_m}$ of 0.33
each, so the run-level $\hat\tau^2$ is zero and the levels collapse to
their mean (shifts of −0.22 to +0.12 nats). The four Student runs have
levels spread over 2.2 nats with standard deviations of about 0.3, so
$\hat\tau^2$ is 1.3, the factors are 0.92 to 0.95 and the shifts are
within 0.08 nats.

## 8. What it does and does not fix

Worst and mean, over the six noisy targets, of the median absolute
bias over subsets, by $M$; the two noiseless targets are within 0.09
nats for every estimate.

| estimate | worst, $M$ = 2 / 3 / 4 / 5 / 8 / 16 | mean, $M$ = 2 / 3 / 4 / 5 / 8 / 16 |
|---|---|---|
| raw | 0.77 / 0.84 / 1.07 / 1.04 / 1.16 / 1.31 | 0.37 / 0.39 / 0.45 / 0.50 / 0.58 / 0.74 |
| cap (the headline today) | 0.95 / 0.97 / 1.17 / 1.26 / 1.39 / 1.78 | 0.30 / 0.29 / 0.35 / 0.37 / 0.37 / 0.45 |
| within-run, full covariance | 0.57 / 0.60 / 0.83 / 0.75 / 0.79 / 0.94 | 0.26 / 0.25 / 0.30 / 0.31 / 0.32 / 0.40 |
| two-level, full covariance | 0.52 / 0.58 / 0.72 / 0.67 / 0.67 / 0.77 | 0.24 / 0.24 / 0.25 / 0.26 / 0.24 / 0.28 |

The two-level estimate is the first whose worst case stays below 0.8
nats at every $M$ and that is acceptable on every target. Its worst
case is the multisensory target at noise 3, where it stays 0.5 to 0.8
nats optimistic; the cap is closer there by 0.09 to 0.32 nats. That
residual is the runs' own bias: a single VBMC run on that target,
scored against its own Monte Carlo ELBO, is already optimistic by
0.74 nats, and shrinking toward a population mean removes selection
within the population and cannot see an error common to all of a
run's components. On that target the GP's own uncertainty is also
miscalibrated (a quarter to two thirds of the components are more
than two standard deviations from the truth), and the method takes
$\Sigma$ at face value. On the Student target the estimate is 0.24
to 0.39 nats pessimistic at $M \le 5$, where the raw value is within
0.11, because the full-covariance form still removes a shared
deviation that is, on that target, mostly real.

Which raises the question of what the target should be. S-VBMC is not
asked to remove the optimism VBMC builds into each run's ELBO, only
not to add to it, so the right yardstick for a stacked estimate is
its bias minus the mean bias of the runs it was built from, each run
scored against its own Monte Carlo ELBO as a stack of one. Medians
over subsets of that **added bias** over the six noisy targets:

| estimate | added bias, $M$ = 2 | $M$ = 5 | $M$ = 16 |
|---|---|---|---|
| raw | +0.02 to +0.15 | +0.13 to +0.36 | +0.27 to +0.60 |
| cap | −0.76 to −0.04 | −1.00 to −0.00 | −1.54 to −0.01 |
| run level alone | +0.01 to +0.10 | +0.10 to +0.20 | +0.21 to +0.46 |
| two-level, full covariance | −0.22 to −0.04 | −0.15 to +0.03 | −0.11 to +0.15 |

The raw value adds optimism that grows with $M$: it tracks the bias
of the input run with the highest reported ELBO, the winner's curse
among runs. The cap removes more than the stacking added, on every
noisy target. The run-level term alone, the one aimed at selection
among runs, removes only a sixth to a half of the addition. The
two-level estimate adds nothing within 0.23 nats at any $M$, and at
small $M$ sits 0.1 to 0.2 nats below its inputs, because its
within-run term also removes part of the runs' own optimism. An
estimator that removes only the stacking's own selection was also
tried: shrink as in section 9, then add back per run what the
shrinkage removes at the run's own weights (weighted by the run's
mass in the stack, or averaged over runs), so that a stack of one run
reports its own ELBO. It halves what the raw value adds and still
adds 0.1 to 0.2 nats at $M$ = 3 to 5, because the run-level term
takes the GP's variance at fixed weights as a level's error and
misses the run-to-run scatter of the runs' own optimism, which is
what the stacking selects on. The two-level estimate ends nearer
zero because its within-run term removes part of that optimism from
every run first.

Two properties are worth keeping in mind. The method has no tuned
constant: $\mu$, $\tau^2$ and $\Sigma$ come from the data of the run.
And it reads nothing beyond `I_sk`, `J_sjk` and the run's own weights,
which every input posterior already carries, so the class's interface
is unchanged and no GP object is needed at stacking time.

## 9. The computation, step by step

For each run $m$ with $K$ components:

1. $I_k = \frac{1}{N_s} \sum_s I_{sk}$ (minus the fixed per-component
   correction);
   $\Sigma_{jk} = \frac{1}{N_s} \sum_s J_{sjk} + \operatorname{Cov}_s(I_{sj}, I_{sk})$,
   the second term the sample covariance across the hyperparameter
   samples; symmetrized.
2. $\hat\mu = \operatorname{mean}(I)$;
   $\nu = (\operatorname{tr} \Sigma - \mathbf{1}^{\top} \Sigma \, \mathbf{1} / K) / (K - 1)$;
   $\hat\tau^2 = \max(\operatorname{Var}(I) - \nu, \, 0)$.
3. $\tilde I = \hat\mu \mathbf{1} + \hat\tau^2 (\hat\tau^2 \mathrm{Id} + \Sigma)^{-1} (I - \hat\mu \mathbf{1})$,
   or $\tilde I = \hat\mu \mathbf{1}$ if $\hat\tau^2 = 0$. One
   $K \times K$ solve.
4. Run level: $E_m = w^{(m)\top} I$ with the run's own weights,
   $V_m = w^{(m)\top} \Sigma \, w^{(m)}$.

Across runs:

5. $\hat\mu_r = \operatorname{mean}(E)$;
   $\hat\tau_r^2 = \max(\operatorname{Var}(E) - \operatorname{mean}(V), \, 0)$;
   $\delta_m = \bigl(\hat\tau_r^2 / (\hat\tau_r^2 + V_m) - 1\bigr) (E_m - \hat\mu_r)$.
6. $\tilde I_k \leftarrow \tilde I_k + \delta_m$ for every component $k$
   of run $m$.

Then, at the weights $w$ the stacking selected, the headline is

$$
\sum_k w_k \tilde I_k + H .
$$

Only the reported value changes; the weights and the posterior are
those of the raw optimization, as with the cap today.
Running the optimization on the shrunken values instead was
tried on the benchmark at $M$ = 3 to 5 and is worse: the optimizer
selects on the shrinkage's own errors (a below-average component
raised toward its run's mean looks better than it is), the headline
comes out 0.1 to 0.2 nats more optimistic than the value-only
shrinkage, and the posterior improves on some targets and worsens on
others. The diagnostic
to report alongside is the noise share, $\nu / s^2$ per run and
mass-weighted over runs: it is 0.2 to 1.1 on the typical noisy
targets, 0.05 on the heavy-tailed one and at most 0.09 on the
noiseless ones, and tells the user how much of the runs' component
spread the GP attributes to its own uncertainty.
